Take-Home Challenge: LLM Inference Performance
The take-home is the primary technical milestone in our interview process and the
stage where we ask you to invest the most focused effort. Subsequent interview rounds
are more conversational and center on system design, your past work, and culture.
Overview
Inco AI is operating at the frontier of inference for the token economics of tomorrow delivering
ultra-eﬃciency across the stack purpose-built for each workload. The take-home is intended to
enable you to highlight your unique abilities to contribute to our mission.
Goal. The goal in this stage is to deliver a meaningful, measurable improvement in performance
or operational behavior for a particular layer of the inference stack (see assigned track).
Deliverable. Deliverables are a pull request (or diff) on your fork or your own repo, and a short
written report or presentation in any format you like, including before and after benchmarking
results.
This is not just a coding question with a single right answer. Instead, we care about the style of
the work, the creativity, and the approach. Use of any material is both allowed and encouraged
(LLMs, agents, papers, blogs, colleagues, etc.). Tell us what you used.
Time budget: Aim for 2–3 days of focused work. If you hit a reasonable blocker that needs
more time, just let us know - extensions are fine.
GPU and token budget. You will receive a virtual credit card which has been pre-loaded with
$100 to spend on compute or tokens. We recommend signing up for Modal which includes $30
of free credits; Modal also uses per-second instant pricing helping you to maximize your credits.
However, you may use any vendor on the allowed list: Modal, AWS, Google AI Studio, Google
Cloud, Google Colab, Lambda, Lightning AI, Azure, Vast.AI, and runpod.io. Please reach out if
you need additional resources or assistance.
Evaluation criteria (not exhaustive): creativity, problem-solving approach, introspection
(especially for ideas that didn't work), ability to ingest and learn new materials; delivering actual
performance improvements is only secondary. Negative results with an insightful presentation
can be as meaningful as performance improvements.

Suggested workflow
1. Pick a concrete workload and state it precisely: model plus input/output lengths, the
task set, or the cluster topology and sustained traffic profile.
2. Set up a benchmark you trust before you change anything: use it as a repeatable
baseline to ground your work. Make sure your baseline is already at a reasonable level.
Optimizing over a trivial baseline is not the goal.
3. Analyze the workload: optimizations can take many different shapes or forms. Try to
understand the workload more deeply and formulate some hypotheses for optimization.
4. Implement at least one meaningful optimization. This is the core step. Be creative
and try different ideas.
5. Re-measure with the benchmark and check quality or operational safety.
Understand the impact of your optimization. Note that some optimizations may also be
lossy, which can be acceptable if the cost-accuracy tradeoff justifies it.
Deliverables
- A PR (or diff/patch): your engine fork, scripts that produce the optimized model, harness
repo, or cluster configuration and deployment code.
- Your benchmark or evaluation harness and the exact commands, so we could re-run it.
- A short report or presentation covering how you scoped the project within your assigned
track, how you set up benchmarking, what your analysis showed, what you tried, what
worked and what didn't, before/after results, and a quality, reliability, or safety
sanity-check. Whatever format you like and makes you feel most comfortable covering in
a one-hour presentation.
Looking forward to seeing what you come up with.

Track A: Engine & Model
Setup
Fork vLLM or SGLang (easiest starting points; TensorRT-LLM is fine if you prefer it). Pick a
small, mainstream, single-GPU model, e.g., Gemma 4, Qwen3.5, Phi-4. This helps save GPU
resources. A less well-known model is also fine, and may be less optimized, i.e. more
headroom. Get the server running and confirm the standard perf features, such as CUDA
graphs and overlap scheduler, are enabled.
Benchmark
vLLM server + aiperf, or your engine's equivalent. Produce a Pareto curve across
concurrencies: x-axis tokens/s/user (latency), y-axis tokens/s/gpu (throughput). Easiest setup:
launch the server once at max concurrency / max batch size, then sweep concurrency in the
client. If you touch the model itself, also baseline a quality eval up front (MMLU-Pro, GSM8K,
HumanEval, IFEval, or your own task set) and report the trade-off explicitly, e.g. "−0.4pp on eval
X for 1.8× throughput and 45% less memory."
Profile
nsys traces, to see what the model is actually doing on the GPU. Useful trick for forcing a sync
somewhere obvious: drop in something like t.sum().item() (you'll likely need to disable CUDA
graphs for that). Swapping kernels also gives good insight into how to read traces.
Potential Directions
- Kernel fusion in Triton or Cuda.
- Hunting GPU bubbles: syncs, PyTorch dispatch overhead, anything leaving the GPU
idle.
- Compiler and graph features: whether torch.compile or piecewise CUDA graphs can
be turned on for prefill, and what the model needs to support them.
- Runtime improvements: scheduler heuristics, chunked prefill tuning, prefix caching,
KV-cache layout and paging, batching policy, attention backend selection per shape,
async CPU/GPU overlap, multi-modal data flow.
- Quantization: weight-only (AWQ / GPTQ), FP8, MXFP4 / NVFP4, or KV-cache; which
layers you keep in high precision, how you pick the calibration set, and whether the
quantized kernel is actually faster at your batch size.
- Speculative decoding: a draft model, an EAGLE or MTP head, or cheap n-gram /
prompt lookup; report acceptance rate alongside speedup.
- Post-training: distillation or a LoRA fine-tune of a smaller model to match the big one on
your target task; pruning, layer dropping, KV-head sharing.
